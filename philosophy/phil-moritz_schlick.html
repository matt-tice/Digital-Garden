Moritz Schlick

References

Summary

Originally trained in physics. At the time (late 19th century), physicists were almost always interested also in philosophy.

Inspirations

Helmholtz

Influenced by Kant

Handuch der physiologischen Optik (1856)

Signs: Perceptions are signs — placeholders for what they signify. At the same time, they don't actually resemble the signifieds in any way.

The structures of signs tells us about the corresponding structure of their signifieds. This implies a causal link in perception that is antithetical to Kant's

  • for Kant, there were no unobservable causes of observable phenomena

On the Facts of Perception

Helmholtz changed his mind, to make his thoughts more Kantian.

Rather than the signs being caused by unseen signifieds, what we really understand when looking at signs is our own perceptions. We then make a conceptual leap, interpretting it as saying something about the underlying sources, even though it isn't.

Planck

Influenced by Kant and by Helmholtz

Signs: For Planck, signs didn't tell us directly about the external world, but rather give us something to interpret.

Relations: We cannot know the inherent nature of things, only the complex web of relations connecting things together. Planck's theory of knowledge is fundamentally structuralist

The Unity of the Physical Universe (1908)

An argument against Ernst Mach's phenomenalistic and empiricist philosophy of science.

Mach didn't believe in underlying physical objects.

Objective: For Planck, physical objects are objective, in the Kantian sense, because they embody the lawfulness of appearances

Allgemeine Erkenntnislehre (1918)

Axioms: Schlick looked to the work of Pasch, Hilbert, and Poincaré to borrow the idea of defining a concept by axioms. Those mathematicians were looking at non-euclidean geometry, and thus modifying the underlying axioms. By modifying the axioms, they viewed themselves as altering the meaning of the relevant terms. For example, "point" changes its meaning, depending on the underlying axioms that set up the geometry.

For Schlick, concepts are imaginary things (Gedankendinge), which allow us to designate objects for our cognition. Like how latitiude and longitude allow you to designate position

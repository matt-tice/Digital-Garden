Differential Operators

If we view d as a kind of linear differential operator, or a system of equations, then

  • the cycles are homogeneous solutions: d(f) = 0.
  • the boundaries are integrable: f = d(g).

The homology then tells you about the non-integrable solutions.

Example: Electromagnetism

In EM, Maxwell's equations tell us ∇ċ B = 0 So the magnetic field is a cycle. But when doing calculations, we often want to integrate the magnetic field, in order to compute things about it; so we need B to be integrable

  • so we want it to be a boundary.

This means that we need B = ∇ × A This says that there is a magnetic potential A, that generates the actual magnetic field.

In fact, looking at the de Rham complex, Helmholtz's theorem says that for RN, the (co)homology at the level of the magnetic field is zero (every magnetic field satisfying Maxwell's equations is integrable).

  1. Aharonov-Bohm Effect

    When you puncture R, you get non-trivial cohomology

    • so there are certain obstructions (non-integrable solutions).
    • In particular, there are fields with zero curl that aren't gradients.

Cohomology

Coboundaries are flat or path-independent or conservative

  • if you take any two paths from the same starting point to the same ending point, you get the same result (of taking the integral over the path, say)
  • so there's no energy being lost anywhere, in a certain sense

Using Poincaré duality, we have that <d f , γ> = <f, ∂ γ> where d is the codifferential, and ∂ is the differential, and the pairing is integrating the function (cochain) over the space (chain).

A coboundary is g = df. So for coboundaries <g, γ> = <f, ∂γ> So g is determined by just the boundary (paths through the bulk are irrelevant).

A cocycle will satisfy <f, ∂ γ> = <0, γ> = 0 for all γ. So it has no information on the boundary

  • it's "path independent" on the boundary, in a higher dimensional sense
  • any two half-coverings of the boundary give the same integral

But think about what this means: when we take two half-coverings, we're just taking two pieces of the boundary, and saying they stitch together to form the whole boundary (they don't literally need to have the same size, any 2-block partition will work). So there's a glue-line that you're stitching the coverings together on.

But no matter how you move, stretch, contract, etc. the covering pieces, the fact that they come together at the glue-line means they enclose a bulk — they're the boundary of something. So the integral of each piece will always be the same! So if you take just one of the two coverings, no matter how you move it around, its integral will never change, as long as you keep the glue-line fixed in place.

  • this is absolutely fascinating!

By the way, you need to keep the glue-line the same, because if you don't one will be bigger than the other, and so you can't stitch the two versions together to enclose a bulk.

But note that here, this only works when we're integrating over ∂ γ. So by assumption there's a bulk, γ. In other words, a cocycle is something that, if you have a bulk, becomes path independent on the boundary.

  • so the cocycle adds no information to the bulk
  • this in turn means that the only way you can get non-trivial info from the bulk is if the actual geometry of the bulk is non-trivial (for example, there's a hole in it)

Cocycles can suck the information from the boundary into the bulk. The boundary data is trivial; all that matters is what's in the bulk

Mapping Cones

A mapping cone of a function f: X → Y glues a cone-version of X onto Y, along f. You first form the mapping cylinder X × I, and then glue an end on Y, and then collapse the other end to a point.

Our perspective on cocycles then tells us that a cocycle's integral won't change, as you deform X, as long as you keep f fixed.

Cohomology

Cohomology is then very interesting: think of the mapping cone, or half a sphere. Being a cocycle for the hemisphere means you can deform the hemisphere and nothing changes.

But that hemisphere is actually the bulk for the gluing line! Which means that deforming the hemisphere also won't change coboundaries on the gluing line.

Consider the pair of pants cobordism M. We break it into 3 parts:

  1. the 1d rims Γ = ∂ Σ
  2. the 2d surface Σ = ∂ M
  3. the 3d bulk M

What we found above is that the cocycles and coboundaries don't notice the shape of the cobordism at all, other than the fact that it has a certain number of rims.

  • in other words, they only care about the topological class of the cobordism
  • this is how we see a topological quantum field theory arising: taking path integrals is independent of the world-sheet defining the cobordism

Chern Classes

The first Chern class is a 2-cocycle So it's something that has no boundary data

  • it can't see boundaries of 3d objects

Topological String Theory

In the A-model you basically work with a pair of pants where you collapse all three of the rims to points

  • this is the virtual holomorphic sphere

The theory is topological in the sense that d ω = 0 (ω is a cycle), for an appropriate d

  • this says that you can change the metric of your pants
  • and even if you do that, you have certain Gromov-Witten invariants (the integral on the surface, of ω)

The idea of quantum cohomology is that you study maps f: S2 → X that hit three different cycles in the manifold X (the collapsed rims of the pants)

You think of the map f as a kind of non-local communication between the cycles (strings)

Summary

Consider a polynomial algebra k[x]. We can perform a resolution on the algebra k[x]/x

  • We want k[x]/x to be the zeroth homology, and for all the other homologies to be zero.

Set C0 = k[x] and C1 = k[x, ε], where ε is a formal element in degree 1, and defining d(ε) = x.

If we further require the algebra structure to be graded commutative, we have ε2 = 0. So we can interpret this as something like an infinitesimal

Now the elements in degree 1 have the form p(x) ε , where p(x) ∈ C0 is a polynomial with boundary d(p) ε + p d(ε) = 0 + p(x) x.

It follows that if there is a cycle in degree 1, we'd have d(p ε) = p(x) x = 0.

If there are no zero-divisors, this implies that there are no cycles. So H1  ≅ 0.

Similarly, if we take the higher degrees, we're trivial, because they would need to be built out of elements in degree 1 (to increase the degree). But since ε2 = 0, this produces no elements above degree 1. Thus Hn  ≥ 1  ≅ 0, and so we get a (very easy) resolution of k[x]: 0 → k[x, ε] → k[x]  → 0 which is quasi-isomorphic to k[x] (as a trivial chain complex: 0 → k[x]  → 0).

Functional Analysis

Fredholm Operators

By definition, these are operators with

  • finite dimensional kernel
  • finite dimensional and closed cokernel

The Fredholm index is dim(ker) - dim(coker) It counts solutions to equations

Atiyah-Singer Index Theorem

If T is an elliptic differential operator on a manifold, the analytical index is equal to the topological index

  • the analytical index is the homology of the operator
  • the topological index is the cohomology of the manifold's tangent bundle.

Harmonic Analysis

A locally compact group is amenable if and only if its first Hochschild cohomology group of its convolution algebra with coefficients in dual modules is zero: H1(L1(G), X*)  ≅ 0

Complex Analysis

Hartog's Extension Theorem

Holomorphic functions are those that are locally complex differentiable

  • each point has some neighbourhood on which it's complex differentiable.

This is enough to conclude that the function is analytic

Sheaf Cohomology

Analytic Continuation

This is actually related to relative cohomology

Mikio Sato viewed real functions (which you'd like to analytically continue) as being boundaries between two holomorphic functions

You build relative cohomology out of this idea: take … → O(C) → O(C ∖ R) → H1R(C, O)  → 0 where O is the sheaf of holomorphic functions. This is a long exact sequence.

An element of O(C ∖ R) is really a pair of holomorphic functions: one defined on the upper half plane, one defined on the lower.

The codifferential into H1 is a computation of the difference between a pair of holomorphic functions, as you approach the real axis.

Functions that admit analytic continuations are precisely coboundaries in the first degree

Wick Rotation

When you take a path integral, say over time, you're integrating ∫-∞∞ ω where ω is some complex differential form.

When we do wick rotation, we want to switch into imaginary time. How does this work?

Consider the upper right and lower left quadrants of the complex plane.

Well say ω is closed (a cocycle). Then we can think of the real and imaginary axes as two half-coverings of a larger bulk made up of the upper right and lower left quadrants

  • if there are no poles in those quadrants, then we can perform wick rotation, because our two different half-coverings give the same integral.

To be a bit more precise, typically what you do is you have to consider finite parts of the quadrants and then take the limits to infinity

  • this is because the integral over the whole, non-compact, domain might not exist

    On the other hand, you can use Borel-Moore Homology

    • this allows your chains to be non-compact.
    • it'll basically give you exactly what I was saying above

I guess another way of thinking about it, is you compactify C (say, into the Riemann sphere) and then play the game

  • the real and imaginary axes become circles on the sphere that meet at 0 and ∞ (north and south poles) But when you do this, your integrand actually becomes very messy at ∞
    • but you can't just remove that point, because it'll add a new point to your boundary
  • so you basically turn the point into a small circle and then take the limit as you close the circle

If the pole at ∞ is regular or logarithmic, everything is fine

  • if it's an essential singularity, you have to be careful
  • this is actually related to global D-Module theory

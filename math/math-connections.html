Summary

When we work with vector bundles, there is no built-in way to add vectors in two different fibers (for example, in two different tangent spaces). This makes defining derivatives of sections of the bundle a non-starter.

This is an important distinction, because it's perfectly possible to define derivatives of functions on a differentiable manifold (f : M → ℝ) — you pull back the strucure to Euclidean space. Given any curve on the manifold γ : ℝ → M, you just take the derivative of the composition f ∘ γ.

The reason this works is because the function f is mapping into just one space. We can actually interpret what's happening as there being a trivial bundle M × ℝ → M, which has the same fiber everywhere. One really nice property is that for trivial bundles you can always do this — there's always a canonical connection called a flat connection which views all the fibers as being the same

Connections are, by definition, a way to interact vectors in two different fibers

Definitions

Tensor product of vector bundles

Let E, F → M be vector bundles. The tensor bundle E ⊗ F → M is the vector bundle whose fiber over each point is Ex ⊗ Fx.

Direct sum of vector bundles

Let E, F → M be vector bundles. The direct sum (or Whitney sum) E ⊕ F → M of these bundles is the vector bundle whose fiber over each point is Ex ⊕ Fx.

Connection

Let π : E → M be a real vector bundle, and let Γ(E) be the space of sections on the bundle E. A connection is a linear map ∇ : Γ(E) → Γ(T*M⊗E) such that ∇(fs) = df ⊗ s + f∇s for all functions f and sections s

Intuition

Given a section of the tensor bundle ∇f : M → T*M ⊗ E, we have a sum of tensors of vectors at every point on the manifold: (∇f)x ∈ T*Mx ⊗ Ex.

The cotangent space allows us to take in vectors and spit out numbers: (∇f)x(v) ∈ Ex, for all v ∈ TxM.

Thinking about this in terms of pairings, (∇f)x(v) is taking the pairing of v with the first tensor factor of (∇f)x and then leaving the second tensor factor alone.

Parallel transport

Let ∇ be a connection on a bundle E → M and s ∈ Γ be a section. Then for any curve γ : [0,1] → M we say that s is parallel along γ if ∇γ′s = 0.

Note

For all γ and for all v ∈ E, there is a unique parallel sγ, v such that sγ, v(0) = v.

The parallel transport map τγ : Ex → Ey is defined by τγ(v) = sγ, v(1).

Holonomy groups

Let E → M be a bundle with connection. For any point x ∈ M, the holonomy group at x is Holx = {τγ|γ is a loop based at x}.

Curvature

Let E → M be a bundle with connection. The curvature of the connection is a two-form F∇ ∈ Γ(⋀2T*M⊗E*⊗E) defined by F∇(X,Y)(s) = ∇X∇Ys − ∇Y∇Xs − ∇[X,Y]s for all tangent vector fields X, Y : M → TM and sections s ∈ Γ(E).

Vertical bundle

Let π : E → M be a smooth fiber bundle. The vertical bundle is ker (dπ) of the map dπ : TE → TM

Intuition

Ehresmann connection

Let π : E → M be a smooth fiber bundle. An Ehresmann connection (or horizontal bundle) is a subbundle H of TE such that TE ≅ H ⊕ ker (dπ)

Intuition

These are connections that we can define on any fiber bundle — not just vector bundles.

Examples

Trivial bundles

Let M × ℝk → M be a trivial bundle. Then we can pick a global basis of sections e1, …, ek.

The trivial (flat) connection ∇0 is defined by ∇v0ei = 0 for all tangent vectors v.

We then can compute the general action of the directional derivative by ∇v0s = ∑(v⋅si)ei

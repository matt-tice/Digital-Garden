ends and coends

References

Summary

Coends basically have the following shape: you take an algebraic structure (covariant), and a geometric structure (contravariant) and you glue things together to form a global structure.

Definitions

Ends

Let F : 𝒞op × 𝒞 → 𝒱 be an enriched functor, where 𝒱 is a symmetric monoidal category and 𝒞 is 𝒱-enriched.

Consider the product over all objects in 𝒞, ∏AMor(A,A).

A wedge w → F is an object w and a collection of maps wA : w → F(A,A) such that the following commutes

w → F(A,A) ↓ ↓ F(B,B) $ \xrightarrow{(f,1)}$ F(A,B)

for all f : A → B.

An end is a universal wedge

Intuition

Ends are ways of taking two kinds of structure and restricting to those pieces which behave the same in both structures.

Natural transformations: These are the main example. The two types of structure are precomposition and postcomposition

Think of the maps wA : w → F(A,A) as something like the maps that make up a natural transformation

Because your end is a limit, you're basically looking at a collection {wA} such that they are invariant under altering the components of F(A, B) independently

Coends

F(A, A) $\xrightarrow{(1,f)}$ F(A, B) ↓ ↓ F(B, A) → w

We denote the coend by ∫A F(A, A)

Intuition

Unlike an end, which restricts to compatible structure; the coend glues two different kinds of structure together

Geometric realization: Glues together the simplicial set blueprint with actual physical simplices

Tensor modules: Glues together two different modules

The maps wA : F(A,A) → w define your gluing

You essentially glue together the maps that change the components of F(A, B) independently.

This is meant to be something like the tensor product of modules

  • In M ⊗ N, you have αm ⊗ n = m ⊗ αn

Similarly, here you want to think of C (its morphisms) as your ring, and the functors as your modules.

Left Kan Extensions

If we have F: C → D and K: C → E

C → D ↓ E

The left Kan extension of F along K is

(LK F)[Y] = ∫X Mor(K(X), Y) ċ F(X)

where ċ is the copwoer

Yoneda

∫A Mor(A,B) ⊗ F(A) ≅ F(B)

∫A Hom(Mor(B, A), F(A)) ≅ F(B)

Examples

Geometric realization coends

Algebra: The simplex functor Δ → Top that assigns a physical simplex to each combinatorial simplex.

Geometry: We look at a fixed simplicial set X : Δop → Set.

Tensor product of modules coends

Take a ring viewed as an abelian-enriched one object category.

Algebra: A left R-module N : R → Ab

Geometry: A right R-module M : Rop → Ab

M⊗RN = ∫RM × N.

Kan extensions coends

Algebra: Some functor that carries algebraic data, which you'd like to extend to a larger space G : 𝒞 → ℰ

Geometry: A hom-functor Mor (F(−),X) : 𝒞op → Set

The left Kan extension is ∫c ∈ 𝒞Mor(F(c),X) × G(c).

Group actions coends

If G acts on a topological space X, you often want to look at the orbit space X/G.

Algebra: We take a trivial map from the delooping of G to the singleton. BG → Top.

Geometry: We take the map BGop → Top that sends the unique object to the space X, and the maps to a right action.

The orbit space is the coend X/G = ∫BGX ×  * .

Sieves ends

Consider the category of open sets of a manifold

Algebra: A functor assigning smooth functions to each open set Open(M) → Alg

Geometry: The open sets Open (M)op → Top

The end takes all of the open sets and produces the global functions that can be appropriately restricted to each local open set.

Definitions

Ultrafilters: Compactness

A space is compact if and only if every ultrafilter on it converges to at least one point.

Intuition

Really, every ultrafilter will always converge to something — the thing is, it just might not be in your space; it might be a kind of infinite point. The ultrafilter definition of compactness then says that no limits escape to infinity

Ultrafilter Lemma

The problem is, how do we know a space even has an ultrafilter on it? The ultrafilter lemma tells you that every space does, but it's equivalent to the axiom of choice.

Theorems

Continuous Maps Preserve Compactness

Let f : A → B be continuous. Then if K ⊆ A is compact, so is f(K).

Take an open cover f(K) ⊆ ⋃αUα. Then pulling everything back, we have K ⊆ f−1f(K) ⊆ f−1(⋃αUα) = ⋃αf−1(Uα). We get a finite subcover, since K is compact, and then push that forward: f(K) ⊆ f(⋃if−1(Ui)) = ff−1(⋃iUi) ⊆ ⋃iUi, getting a finite subcover for f(K).

Tychonoff's Theorem

Let {Cα} be an arbitrary collection of compact spaces. Then ∏αCα is compact

Set Theory

Tychonoff's theorem is equivalent to the axiom of choice

Inside compact: Closed ⊆ Compact

T2: Compact ⊆ Closed

Counterexamples

  1. Zariski Topology

    Consider the Zariski topology on ℝ: the only closed sets are finite points and the whole space.

    Every subset is compact

    • proper open sets are precisely those subsets which have a finite number of points removed from the whole space.
    • given any subset, and an open cover A ⊆ ⋃αFαc = (⋂αFα)c, there will only be a finite number of points missing in the cover (since you're taking the intersection of a collection of finite sets): A ⊆ ⋃αFαc = Fc.

    You can then just pick one set for each of these missing points, and that gives you a finite subcover.

    Now the set (0,1) is compact but not closed.

Maps: Compact → T2 are Closed

Let f : A → B map from a compact space to a Hausdorff one. Then f is closed

Let C ⊆ A be closed. Then it is also compact, and so it gets mapped to a compact subset, which is then closed, since B is T2.

Continuous Bijections: Compact → T2 are Homeos

Maps: Compact → T2 are Closed tells us that our morphism is closed. We can use this to show that f−1 is continuous:

Let C ⊆ A be closed. Then (f−1)−1(C) = f(C) is closed.

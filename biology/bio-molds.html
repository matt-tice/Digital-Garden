Summary

Molds are types of fungi that belong (mainly) to the phylas Ascomycota or Zygomycota

Individual spores will germinate and grow outward at an equal rate in all directions. Over time the circles will merge into irregular shapes

Because of the aerial hyphae used in sporulation, it almost always looks fuzzy, velvety, cottony, hairy, or powerdery.

Colour-gradient: The pigment is contained in the conidia, so young mold will actually be white or colourless. This causes mold to have a colour-gradient — the outermost mold is light or translucent

Structure

Hyphae: A microscopic, tube-like filament. They branch out like roots to gather more food from a source. What we see macroscopically as mold is really just a huge aggregate of hyphae!

Mycelium: The network that hyphae form

Conidiophores: The stalks/stems that mold produces, which holds the spores. Each conidiophore is topped with conidia. The conidiophores are what gives the mold its fuzzy texture.

Conidia: The spores of the mold.

Spores: When environmental conditions shift, causing a drop in moisture or nutrients, the mycelium transitions to a reproductive phase, in which it aerializes hyphae called conidiophores or sporangiophores that contain the spores of the mold, called conidia.

Colour: The pigment of the mycelium is inside of the conidia.

Growth

Germination: The dehydrated spore first absorbs free water. It then has to break down sugars to have the energy to grow

Apical: Hyphae grow from their tips; this is called apical growth. They produce exoenzymes to break down organic compounds like cellulose, so they can absorb the nutrients

Water activity: The amount of unbound water molecules available. Pure distilled water was aw = 1, fresh bread has aw ≈ 0.97. Most molds need at least aw = 0.8 to germinate

Types

Ascomycota

Members of this phylum are commonly called sac fungi or ascomycetes.

Septate hyphae: The hyphae are divided into little rooms by cross-walls called septa. Each septum has a central pore that allows cytoplasm through (and sometimes nuclei)

Ascus: This is the defining feature of this phylum. Asci are special spore-bearing cells. The spores they produce are called ascospores

Ascospores: Each ascus produces usually eight ascospores.

Examples

  1. Penicillium

    Highly acid-tolerant. That's why you're likely to find it in tomato sauce, where many bacteria wouldn't like the environment

    1. Penicillium chrysogenum

      Growth: Dense, flat, velvety colonies

      Colour: Starts white, turns blue-green

      Structure: Conidiophores branch out symmetrically to form a structure that looks like a broom

  2. Aspergillus

    The chief competitor to penicillium in food spoilage

    They often start bright yellow and then become browner as they mature.

    1. Aspergillus niger

      Commonly confused with Stachybotrys

      Structure: Single smooth stalks terminate in large globes called vesicles, which are covered in asci. Microscopically it looks like a field of dandelions, where the dandelions are the condidae.

  3. Stachybotrys chartarum

    Appearance: Slimy; targets complex cellulose fibers and requires materials to be continuously soaked for weeks.

    Structure: Dark septate hyphae. Conidiophores end in clusters of ellipse-shaped cells (phialides) that look like a closed fist

  4. Cladosporium

    Olive green or brown patches

  5. Alternaria

    Fuzzy, dark brown or black

  6. Aureobasidium pullulans

    Slimy pinkish-white film that matures into dark brown/black

Zygomycota

Aseptate hyphae: The hyphae are wide hollow tubes. Thousands of nuclei share the same space. This allows rapid sharing of nutrients. As a consequence, these molds grow incredibly fast.

Water: Require incredibly high water activity to grow

Zygosporangium: Inside of this dark, thick-walled structure, two compatible gametangia fuse to form a zygospore. This fusion process is called karyogamy

Zygospores: Each zygosporangium produces a single large zygospore. They are typically produced when the zygosoprangium is physically ruptured

Examples

  1. Rhizopus stolonifer

    This is common bread mold

    Appearance: White fuzzy circles with dark centers

    Structure: Has an interesting root structure — there are horizontal hyphae called stolons that rapidly claim territory over a surface like bread. Then at the base of the conidiophores there are filaments that look like tree roots, called rhizoids. The black dots in the center of the white are the mature, spore-filled sporangia.

    Heat: The internal temperature of bread when baked is around 95 celsius, and vegetative cells and asexual sporangiospores of rhizopus die around only 60 celsius.

  2. Mucor mucedo

    Appearance: Exceptionally tall, fluffy mats (can be several centimeters!)

    Structure: No rhizoids. Sporangiophores hold single globular sporangia that break open even under a breath

  3. Pilobolus

    Grows on herbivore manure

    Structure: Sub-sporangial vesicle that is light-sensitive. It can literally launch itself up to two meters towards the sunlight

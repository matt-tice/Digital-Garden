Summary

Hormones

At the beginning of your period, both estrogen and progesterone are low.

Follicle-Stimulating Hormone

When your period starts, your brain releases Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH) which signals to the ovaries to start maturing eggs.

Estrogen

Estrogen is produced by the egg follicles. It causes the endometrium to thicken with blood vessels and tissue.

It also creates a thin cervical mucus to aid in sperm's travel

Luteinizing Hormone

Produced by the pituitary gland.

LH Surge

The sudden spike in LH triggers ovulation.

Ovulation: This is the process of the most dominant follicle bursting open to release an egg. Ordinarily, only the single most dominant follicle opens.

Estrogen signals the body to grow the uterine lining, mature the egg, and trigger ovulation

SA/DA: Estrogen actually boosts serotonin and dopamine

Skin: Estrogen promotes collagen production, keeping your skin hydrated

Progesterone

Once ovulation occurs, the empty follicle transforms into a gland called the corpus luteum, which creates progesterone

Endometrium: Progesterone is the signal to stop growing new tissue in the endometrium. The tissue that's already there starts to get spongier

Calm: Progesterone calms the nervous system and promotes deep sleep.

Slow digestion: Progesterone also slows digestion, allowing you to absorb more nutrients from the same amount of food.

Period: After about two weeks, the corpus luteum dies off, ceasing the flow of progesterone. Without the progesterone, the uterine lining can't be maintained, and so breaks down, and this is your period.

Oily: Progesterone stimulates sebum glands

Temperature: Increasing progesterone also increases your body temperature

Fertilization

If an egg is fertilized, it travels down the fallopian tube to get to the uterine lining. This takes about a week.

Once it gets to the lining, it implants itself and its outermost cells enter into the lining. These cells are called the chorionic cells, and they eventually become the placenta

Once the egg implants into the lining, it starts producing Human Chorionic Gonadotropin (hCG)

Human Chorionic Gonadotropin

Chorion: This is the outermost membrane of a developing embryo. Once the egg is implanted into the endometrium, the chorion starts developing into the placenta. The chorion comes from the trophoblasts of the blastocyst (the ball of cells that the fertilized egg turns into)

Syncytiotrophoblast: The trophoblast cells form this outer layer, which ends up producing hCG into the bloodstream

Pregnancy tests: This is what they're testing for.

Corpus luteum: hCG tells the corpus luteum to stick around and increase its production of progesterone. This prevents the uterine lining from collapsing (and thus you don't get a period)

The corpus luteum can only survive for another 7 to 10 weeks before finally dying off. During this time the placenta develops, and produces its own progesterone! This is called the luteal-placental shift

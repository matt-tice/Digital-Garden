Summary

Coolants

Water-cooled

This is the most common reactor type

Max temp: 350C Efficiency: 32–34% (for heat to electricity conversion)

Advantages

Everyone knows how to make them Easy to acquire parts Regulations are streamlined Water is easy to work with

Disadvantages

Water has a low boiling point Typically you want the reactor to work above 300C In order to use water, you need to operator the reactor at a very high pressure PV = nRT

The high pressure requires more expensive pressure vessels, and increases the danger of leaks

Pressurized Water Reactor (PWR)

Water is kept at 150x atmospheric pressure Water is the neutron moderator

Reactor must be shutdown while refueling

Pressurized Heavy Water Reactor (PHWR)

Uses heavy water as coolant and moderator Heavy water absorbs fewer neutrons, allowing natural uranium to be used as fuel

Reactor can be refueled while it operates (continuous refueling)

Boiling Water Reactor (BWR)

Water is kept at 75x ap

Water is allowed to boil, and is sent directly to the turbine — there's no secondary coolant

The water also serves as the neutron moderator

Reactor must be shut down while refueling

Light Water Cooled Graphite Moderated Reactors (LWGR)

Water coolant boils and is primary coolant

Graphite is the neutron moderator; it absorbs fewer neutrons

Continuous refueling

Gas-cooled

Can be operated at a higher temperature This leads to higher energy conversion efficiency

Max temp: 800–850 C Efficiency: 40–50%

CO2 or He is used for coolant Graphite neutron moderator

Metal-cooled

Low pressure

Coolants: Na, Pb, lead-bismuth

These coolants aren't good at moderating neutrons So the core has a lot of fast neutrons

These fast neutrons can actually be used to create new fuel

Disadvantages

Lead-bismuth creates radioactive material Lead / lead-bismuth are heavy and corrosive at high temperatures

Salt-cooled

High temp allowed, high efficiency Low pressure

Plant Design

The reactor vessel contains the core

The reactor core contains the nuclear fuel Primary coolant is circulated through the core. The heat goes into the coolant, and is transferred to the steam generator

The steam generator is a dense bundle of metal tubes surrounded by the secondary coolant (typically water) The 2nd coolant boils, producing steam, which goes into a turbine connected to a generator

The turbine turns, uses EM induction to produce electricity, which goes to the power grid

The steam that exits the turbine is sent into a condenser, converting it back into water, which is then sent back into the 2nd coolant area

The cooling of the condenser is provided by an external water source, typically a river, lake, or ocean. But it can also be done using cooling towers, which cool using the atmosphere

Control rods absorb neutrons. By pulling them in/out of the core, you control the amount of fission happening

Neutron moderators can slow down the speed of neutrons, turning them into thermal neutrons. This can help the fuel work more efficiently. Reactors that have these moderators are called thermal reactors

Uranium needs thermal neutrons, but something like plutonium doesn't Plutonium reactors are called fast reactors

Fast reactors actually can't use water as a primary coolant, because it slows the neutrons too much

Summary

Measures the raw operating cash profit generated by a company from its core business, prior to costs, taxes, and accounting write-offs

Earnings

This is also called net income or the bottom line

Definition: The final profit a company keeps after subtracting expenses, interest payments, and taxes from its total revenue

CFO: (Cash Flow from Operations) This should be greater than or equal to the net income — non-cash charges like depreciation get added back into cash flow

Computation

Revenue: Total money brought in from selling goods/services

COGS: (Cost of Goods Sold) We subtract this from our revenue. These are the direct costs to make the product — raw materials, factory labour, drug manufacturing

Gross profit: This is Revenue − COGS. It doesn't account for running the corporate office

OpEx: (Operating Expenses) We subtract this. The day-to-day overhead of running the company — salaries, rent, marketing, research and development, and depreciation and amoritization

EBIT: This is also called Operating Income. It's the profit generated purely by the operations of the business

Interest: Subtract this. Interest paid to banks or bondholders on company debt.

Taxes: Subtract this. Corporate income taxes owed to the government

Net income: (Earnings) The official bottom-line profit

Earnings per share

This is literally taking the earnings and dividing it by the total number of shares of the company.

Accounting

Accrual rules: Revenue is recorded when earned. Expenses are recorded when incurred. This creates two different versions of earnings

IFRS: Canadian companies follow the International Financial Reporting Standards in their earnings. The US follows GAAP.

Adjusted earnings: These are what management thinks are an appropriate representation of the actual earnings of the company. Things might be removed, like one-time foreign currency swings, one-off legal settlements, or a gain from selling a building. Some companies exclude stock-based compensation of executives, or restructuring costs, making a company that's losing money appear profitable

Accrual accounting

Unlike Cash Accounting, where you record income once the cash enters your account and expenses when it leaves your account, in Accrual Accounting, things are recorded the moment the service/good is rendered, not when the bill is paid.

  1. Revenue

    Net X: A company will ship goods or finish a contract and then send an invoice with a deadline of 30 days (Net 30) or 60 days (Net 60), etc.

    Because of this, in the accounting, as soon as the service is rendered, the company records its revenue, and adds the amount it's waiting to be paid to Accounts Receivable

    When the bill is actually paid or if it's partially paid, the corresponding amount is transferred from Accounts Receivable to Cash

    Bad Debt Expense: If the customer never pays (example: goes bankrupt) the unpaid invoice is written up as a Bad Debt Expense, which reduces their earnings

    DSO: (Days Sales Outstanding) The average number of days it takes the company to collect cash after making a sale

  2. Expenses

    For the company that purchases the good or service, the dual process happens. But it's slightly different between goods and services

    Services: When a company receives a service, they immediately record the expense, and adds the same amount to Accounts Payable on their balance sheet. The dollar amount is then removed from AP and from cash, when the bill is paid

    Goods: If a company buys goods to resell, they don't put the bill immediately into expenses. Instead, in this case they add the dollar amount to Inventory (an asset) rather than expenses, along with Accounts Payable in the same amount. Then when the bill for the goods is paid, Accounts Payable and Cash decrease, as usual, but there's no expense on the balance sheet. When the merchandise is sold, inventory is reduced in the amount that the inventory cost and COGS is increased by the same amount, and Revenue (+ Cash/Accounts Receivable) is increased by whatever the company was paid by the seller for the goods.

    In other words, once the company sell the inventory, they only subtract the amount that they paid for the inventory when they bought it.

Channel Stuffing

In order to make their earnings higher right before a quarter ends, a company might time certain business moves right around earnings time

For example

  • shipping extra inventory to distributors
  • signing a multi-year software contract and count an unreasonable amount of it as upfront revenue

Accounts receivable: These are unpaid customer bills. If this is growing much faster than revenue, it means the company is booking sales on paper, but not actually collecting cash for it

Capitalizing Costs

Companies might try to write down things they shouldn't as capital assets that they can depreciate, to artificially lower their costs in each quarter. They my try to do this with routine maintenance, software updates, or marketing costs, calling them capital investments

Big Bath

Putting all the bad news into one quarter.

When a new CEO takes over after a bad year, they often write down every single weak asset, inventory pile, or bad project into one bad quarter. The idea is that investors will forgive it since there's a new CEO stepping in to clean up the mess. It then makes the next four quarters have seemingly astounding growth

Cookie-jar reserves

To calculate earnings, management has to do a lot of estimation — how many customers won't pay their bills, how much warranty repair work will cost, what unsold inventory is worth, etc.

Companies might overestimate their expenses in a good year, making it seem like they didn't make quite as much as they really did, so that later, in a bad year, they can underestimate the expenses, to give the earnings a boost

Depreciation

When a company purchases a major facility that it plans to use for decades, it often costs a ton of money. The reason the company is willing to put out all that money is because they expect to get returns on it for many years into the future

But from an accounting perspective, this creates a bit of a problem: if the company just puts the entire expenditure in the year they buy the new asset, they would report a massive loss immediately, and then every subsequent year, they'd not have to account for that cost, making them seem suddenly far more profitable

Matching principle: Instead, the cost is spread out evenly over the life of the asset

Physical: Depreciation accounts for tangible (physical) assets that wear out over time, like pipelines, rail tracks, factories

When a company builds a major facility, they deduct a slice of the construction cost each year

For investors

Depreciation actually serves as a reminder to investors that the company's physical assets wear out over time, and they need to replace them.

You can think of the depreciation as the cost the company has to pay each year to maintain the physical asset. However, in reality, since the asset is already paid for, the company doesn't have to pay any wear-and-tear costs. So you should always check depreciation versus capital expenditures.

If CapEX is much higher than Depreciation the company is pursuing aggressive growth, building new factories, etc.

If they're approximately equal, then the company is building/replacing/maintaining about as much as is being lost by the asset getting older. This is essentially saying the company is in a steady-state

If Depreciation is higher, especially if it's consistent for several years, that suggests that management is under-investing in upkeep to make free cash flow look temporarily strong. This is a big warning sign.

Taxes

Companies are allowed to deduct depreciation and amortization from their taxable profits. This reduces the reported net income, reducing their tax bill, and thus allowing the company to keep more cash

But the way this works differs between the statements given to investors and those given to the government

Investor financial statements

Company management estimates an asset's lifetime based on past experience, industry standards, etc. Independent auditors then have to sign off on the numbers

Companies must also disclose to investors how long they expect the lifetimes to be

Prospective change: If an asset starts wearing out faster than expected, the company will change their estimate for the lifetime and spread the remaining value over the shorter timeline instead. This doesn't rewrite past years, it only impacts things moving forward. This lowers future earnings because the depreciation increases

Impairment charge: If the asset suddenly breaks or becomes obsolete, the company will take an immediate impairment charge (write-down ) to wipe the remaining book value off the Balance Sheet. This creates a huge loss in the current quarter, as all of the remaining depreciation gets sucked out of that single quarter.

Corporate tax returns

Governments assign strict asset categories that have fixed depreciation rates. For example, 30% per year for vehicles or 5% for buildings.

Amoritization

Amoritization is the same matching principle as depreciation, but for intangible assets with finite legal or useful lifetimes, like patents, software, or customer contracts

Depletion

This is the matching principle in the case of natural resources physically extracted from the ground, like oil, ore, uranium

Taxes

The amount paid to the government. This is stripped from EBITDA because tax rates vary wildly across countries and industries, so unless you're familiar with all the details, including the taxes muddies the waters.

EBT: Corporate income taxes are a percentage of Earnings before Taxes.

Tax loss carryforwards: If a young growth company loses $50M over the past five years and then turns profitable, tax laws in Canada and the US allow them to use the past losses as a tax credit

Sector Credits: Tax credits like mining credits, clean-energy subsidies, or research and development credits can slash a company's tax rate to near zero temporarily

Interest tax shield: In most countries, interest payments on debt are tax-deductible. This incentivizes companies to take on debt, since it acts as the government subsidizing part of the borrowing cost

Dividends: These are generally not tax-deductible

Book and Cash Taxes

Because there are two different sets of depreciation rules (the standardized ones for goverment taxes and the audited one for investors), the Income Tax Expense on the Income Statement given to investors often doesn't match the actual cheque that was sent to the government to pay their taxes. But it does end up working out the same over the lifetime of the asset

If management estimates a longer lifetime than the government, they'll have less depreciation each year, so the income tax listed on the Investor Books will be higher than what they actually paid in the first year. But then since the depreciation carries over for longer, in future years on the investor sheet the company's listed tax will be less than what they paid to the government (since they won't be able to use the depreciation on the government tax side, since it'll have already be used up by that point)

Deferred tax liability: The company paid less tax than what's listed on the Investor Books. They record the difference on their Balance Sheet as a Deferred tax liability. In other words, they tell investors we're giving you the average tax we're going to pay per year, but even though this year we actually paid less than it, we're going to have to pay more than average in future years.

A company can try to do this continually — they just keep buying new equipment every year, to roll the tax deferral forward over and over.

When the government offers an accelerated depreciation that lets the company take an amount of their taxes today, compared to if they had to depreciate over a longer period. Because of this, the accelerated depreciation acts like an interest free loan. By buying new equipment each year, the company keeps the amount they owe in taxes lower each year, even though in the end they'll still pay the same amount — they just get a longer time to pay it.

Deferred tax asset: The company paid more tax to the government than what's listed on the Investor Books.

Young companies who lose a lot of money in their early years bank tax-loss carryforwards, which show up on the Balance Sheet as Deferred Tax Assets. Then when the business finally turns around, they'll pay less in taxes for a few years.

Interest

The cost of borrowing money. This is removed from EBITDA so that you can evaluate the business itself, without looking at the debt carried

You can borrow money through things like bank loans corporate bonds, and credit lines.

Interest expense: On the Income Statement, this goes below Operating Income — philosophically, this means that interest isn't treated as an operating cost; it's a financing decision

Interest income: Companies with large cash piles will earn interest on their bank balances. The difference between interest income and interest expense is called Net interest

Impact: Unlike depreciation, which is just an accounting trick, interest is real cash leaving the company each month.

Interest coverage ratio

To determine if a company has too much debt, use the formula

$$ \frac{\text{Operating Income}}{\text{Interest Expense}} $$

Above 5x: Comfortable

Below 2x: Danger

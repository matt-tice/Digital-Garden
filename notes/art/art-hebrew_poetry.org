* Summary

* Language features

** Shoresh

Hebrew is built on three-letter consonant roots, called *shoresh*. This allows a connection between certain words that seem unrelated in english

For example, bitachon (security), bitchah (trust), beten (pregnancy) go together musically and psychologically

** Gender

Every noun is gendered. Poets will use this in anthropomorphizing things like the sun, wind, or Jerusalem.

A man walking through Jerusalem can have this nice structure of he was versus she was

** Alphabet

=Gematria:= Every Hebrew letter is strongly associated with a number. Poets will choose words whose shoresh adds up to a significant number, like 18 (life)

* Biblical poetry

Doesn't use rhyme or strict metre. 

=Parallelism:= The underlying structure of biblical poetry is a balance between *two halves* of a verse. Ideas replace syllables

=Synonym:= The second line reinforces the first

The heavens declare the glory of God
and the sky proclaims his handiwork 

=Antithesis:= The second line demonstrates the opposite of the first

For the Lord watches over the way of the righteous
but the way of the wicked leads to destruction

=Rhythm:= There is usually a triplet rhythm in the language used; it's usually a single line split into two, where one half has three stressed words and the other has two or three

* Golden Age (10 - 12th century)

Combined Hebrew and Arabic poetry

=Meter:= Arabic poetry measures using short versus long syllables. Hebrew doesn't make this distinction, so poets used artificial short syllables like the *sheva vowel* 

=Monorhyme:= Entire poems would end on the same syllable, rather than english poetry that often pairs up endings before switching to new syllables to rhyme

=Muwashshah:= A stanza-based poem (strophic) with a recurring refrain

* Modern Hebrew

When Hebrew was revived as a spoken language, different people spoke it differently. 

=Ashkenazi:= Stressed the penultimate syllable. Poets would use iambic or trochaic rhythms

=Sephardic:= Stressed the final syllable

** Yehuda Amichai

He was born Ludwig Pfeuffer in Germany. His family fled the Nazis when he was 12. He changed his last name to Amichai (my nation lives)

=Military:= Amichai fought in WWII, the 1948 War of Independence, and the wars in 1956 and 1973.

=Colloquial:= Used colloquial language. Dealt with small domestic details

=Religion:= He used biblical references, expressions from the Talmud, and liturgical language alongside modern slang

** Lifting properties

A pair of morphisms \( \ell \dashv r \) have the lifting property (\( \ell \) has the left lifting property with respect to \( r \), and \( r \) has the right lifting property with respect to \( \ell \)) if 

        for all commuting squares \( fr = \ell g \) there is a \( \phi  \) such that \( \ell \phi  = f \) and \( \phi r = g \)

*** Intuition

If we work in the arrow category, we can view commuting squares as morphisms. So we can reformulate this as follows

We say that a pair of objects \( (\ell, r) \) is lifting if for all morphisms \( (f,g) : \ell \rightarrow r \) there is some \( \phi  \) with \( (f, \phi ): \ell \rightarrow 1 \) and \( (\phi , g) : 1 \rightarrow r \)

In my square-bracket notation from [[id:79e306c1-1696-4010-b95a-31c56e1772de][comma categories]], we have \( [g,f] : \ell \rightarrow r \), so that \( \ell g = fr \). Then the lifting property means that we get \( [\phi , f] : \ell \rightarrow 1 \)  and \( [g, \phi ] : 1 \rightarrow r \)

From this perspective, there's a nice homotopy-interpretation: we have that \( \phi  \) is a weak inverse of \( f \) and \( g \) --- it's a weak left inverse of one, and weak right inverse of the other.

In that sense, the lifting morphisms are kind of like morphisms that are contractible (from one side)



** Weak equivalences

Let \( \mathcal{C} \) be a category. A collection of morphisms \( \mathcal{W} \) are called *weak equivalences* if \( \operatorname{Iso}(\mathcal{C}) \subseteq \mathcal{W} \) and if any two of \( \left\{ f, g, fg \right\} \) are in \( \mathcal{W} \), so is the third.

A category and a collection of weak equivalences is called a *category with weak equivalences* 

*** Intuition

Let's look at the three possibilities for things that can be in \( \mathcal{W} \):
\( [g, 1] : f \rightarrow fg \) , \( [1, f] : fg \rightarrow g \), and \( [g, f] : f \rightarrow g \)

So it's something like if we have any of the above maps living in \( \mathcal{W} \) then the composition of the elements in the map also lives in \( \mathcal{W} \).

** Weak factorization systems

A *weak factorization system* on a category \( \mathcal{C} \) is a pair of classes of morphisms \( (\mathcal{L}, \mathcal{R}) \) such that every morphism can be factored as
\[
f: X \xrightarrow{\ell} Y \xrightarrow{r} Z
\]

and \( \mathcal{L}, \mathcal{R} \) are precisely the class of morphisms that have the lifting properties against each other.

*** Intuition

Let's work in the arrow category. The factorization aspect means that for each \( f \) we have some \( [1, \ell] : f \rightarrow r \), which is kind of a weird condition, but I guess it means that every morphism has some homotopy-equivalent morphism

Now say we have \( (f,g) : a \rightarrow b \) and we take a factorization \( [\ell_{2} r_{2}, \ell_{1} r_{1}] : a \rightarrow b \).

We can rewrite this as \( [r_{2} , \ell_{1}] : a \ell_{2} \rightarrow r_{1} b \) , which is then equivalent to \( [r_{1} b, a \ell_{2}] : \ell_{1} \rightarrow r_{2} \). This will then have a lift, so that \( [\phi , a \ell_{2}] : \ell_{1} \rightarrow 1 \) and \( [r_{1} b,  \phi ] : 1 \rightarrow r_{2} \)

Oh maybe we think of this like \( [\phi , a] : \ell_{1} \rightarrow \ell_{2} \) and \( [b, \phi ] : r_{1} \rightarrow r_{2} \).

Then we're saying something like if you map between factorizations \( [b,a] : \ell_{1} r_{1} \rightarrow \ell_{2} r_{2} \) there's always a way to split this up into maps between the left and right parts of the factorizations \( [b,\phi ] : r_{1} \rightarrow r_{2} \) and \( [\phi , a] : \ell_{1} \rightarrow \ell_{2} \)

** Model structure

A *model structure* on a category \( \mathcal{C} \) is a choice of three classes of morphisms called cofibrations \( (C) \), fibrations \( (F) \), and weak equivalences \( (W) \), such that 

        1. \( W \) makes \( \mathcal{C} \) into a category with weak equivalences
        2. \( (C, W \cap F) \) is a weak factorization system
        3. \( (C \cap W, F) \) is a weak factorization system.

If \( \mathcal{C} \) is also complete and cocomplete, we call it a *model category* 

** (co)Fibrant object

Let \( \mathcal{C} \) be a model category. An object \( A \) is *fibrant* if the unique map to the terminal object is a fibration
\[
A \rightarrow *
\]
An object \( B \) is *cofibrant* if the unique map from the initial object is a cofibration
\[
\varnothing \rightarrow B
\]
And if *bifibrant* if it is both fibrant and cofibrant

*** Intuition

**** Fibrant
These things are easy to map /into/, they're basically /cofree/. Once you map into the generators \( \phi : C \rightarrow V \) (each element of \( C \) gets sent to a generator) then you define a  comultiplication to be sent to the factorwise \( \phi  \)


**** Cofibrant

These things are basically /free/ (easy to map out of). Another way of thinking about them, is that they *have no constraints* --- once you map the generators, every other element is a formal multiplication, which becomes a real multiplication in the codomain.




** (co)fibrant replacement

Let \( \mathcal{C} \) be a model category and \( A \) be an object. A *fibrant replacement* is a fibrant object \( RA \) and an acyclic cofibration
\[
A \rightarrow RA
\]

A *cofibrant replacement* is a cofibrant object \( QA \) and an acyclic fibration
\[
QA \rightarrow A
\]


** Retract

A map \( f: A \rightarrow B \) is called a *retract* of \( g: C \rightarrow D \) if there are maps \( f \xrightarrow{[a,b]} g \xrightarrow{[c,d]} f \) such that \( [ac, bd] = [1,1] \)

* Theorems

** (co)Fibrant replacements always exist

Let \( A \) be an object in a model category. Then there exists both a fibrant and cofibrant object.

*** Proof

Since \( (C, W \cap F) \) and \( (C \cap W, F) \) are weak factorization systems, we can factor the maps \( \varnothing \rightarrow A \rightarrow * \) into 
\[
\varnothing \xrightarrow{\ell_{1}} QA \xrightarrow{r_{1}} A  \xrightarrow{\ell_{2}} RA  \xrightarrow{r_{2}} *
\]

where \( \ell_{1} \) is cofibrant, \( r_{1} \) is acyclic fibrant, \( \ell_{2} \) is acyclic cofibrant and \( r_{2} \) is fibrant

** Fibrations are closed under pullbacks

Dually, cofibrations are closed under pushouts

That is, if \( f \in  F \) and we have any pullback square \( [a,\tilde{a}] : \tilde{f} \rightarrow f \) then \( \tilde{f} \in  F \)

*** Proof

To determine if \( \tilde{f} \in  F \), we just need to test that it has the right lifting property against all acyclic cofibrations. Let \( [c,d] : \ell \rightarrow \tilde{f} \) be a commutative square, where \( \ell \in  W \cap C\). We compose to get 
\[
[ca, d\tilde{a}] : \ell \rightarrow \tilde{f} \rightarrow f
\]

Since \( f \in  F \), we have a lift \( [ca, \phi ] : 1 \rightarrow f \) and \( [\phi , d \tilde{a}] : \ell \rightarrow 1 \). So we have 
\[
[a, \phi ] : c \rightarrow f
\].
Then by the universal property of the pullback, we have a map \( \Psi \) with \( \Psi \tilde{f} = c \)  and \( \Psi \tilde{a} = \phi  \). So \( [c, \Psi] : 1 \rightarrow \tilde{f} \). Now we use  \( [\phi , d \tilde{a}] : \ell \rightarrow 1 \) to write \( [\phi , d] : \ell \rightarrow \tilde{a} \). Plugging in our map from above, we have 
\[
\ell \Psi \tilde{a} = d \tilde{a}.
\]
Now we use the uniqueness of the universal map. Since we also have \( \ell \Psi \tilde{f} = \ell c = d \tilde{f} \), we get that \( \ell \Psi = d \), so \( [\Psi, d] : \ell \rightarrow 1 \)

Thus we get our lift of \( [c,d] : \ell \rightarrow \tilde{f} \), so \( \tilde{f} \in  F \).

** \( W \cap F \cap C = \operatorname{Iso} (\mathcal{C}) \)

Let \( \mathcal{C} \) be a model category. Then \( W \cap F \cap C = \operatorname{Iso} (\mathcal{C}) \).

*** Proof

Let \( f \in  W \cap F \cap C \). In particular, it is an acyclic (co)fibration, so we have a lift of
\[
[1,1] : f \rightarrow f
\]
into \( [1, \phi ] : 1 \rightarrow f \) and \( [\phi , 1] : f \rightarrow 1 \). Thus \( \phi  f = 1 = f \phi  \), so \( f \) is an isomorphism.

=Other direction:= Let \( f \) be an isomorphism. Then by definition of weak equivalence, \( f \in  W \). Now take an acyclic fibration \( \ell \) and acyclic cofibration \( r \). Now we look at \( [a,b] : \ell \rightarrow f \) and \( [c,d] : f \rightarrow r \). Now since \( f \) is invertible, we have \( \ell a = bf \) implies \( \ell (a f^{-1} ) = b \) so \( [af^{-1} , b] : \ell \rightarrow 1 \). And \( [a, a f^{-1} ] : 1 \rightarrow f \). So \( f \in F  \).

Similarly, \( [f^{-1} d, d] : f \rightarrow 1 \) and \( [c, f^{-1} d] : 1 \rightarrow r \), so \( f \in C \).

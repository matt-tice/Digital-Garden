* Summary



We take as our equation \(D \phi = \delta \) , where \(D = -\partial^{2}_{t} + \partial^{2}_{x} +  \partial^{2}_{y} +  \partial^{2}_{z} - m^{2}\)

This is where things get really interesting: we plug in a function
\[
\langle \mathcal{F}^{-1} [\phi], \mathcal{F}[Df] \rangle= \langle \phi, Df \rangle = \langle \delta , f \rangle = f(0)
\]
So in general you get something non-zero on the right.

Now we can write \(\mathcal{F}[Df] = (-k_{0}^{2} + k_{1}^{2} + k_{2}^{2} + k_{3}^{2} - m^{2}) \mathcal{F}[f]\). And also
\[
\mathcal{F}[f(x)] = \int e^{-i kx} f(x) dx
\]

\[
f(x) = \mathcal{F}^{-1} \mathcal{F}[f] = (2 \pi )^{-4} \int e^{ikx} \mathcal{F}[f] dk
\]

So \(f(0) = (2 \pi )^{-4} \int \mathcal{F}[f] dk = (2 \pi )^{-4} \langle 1, \mathcal{F}[f]\rangle \), where (importantly!) \(1\) is a *distribution*, not a function. It's a distribution acting on functions in momentum space.

Now we have \(\langle  \mathcal{F}^{-1} [\phi], (-k_{0}^{2} + k\cdot k - m^{2})\mathcal{F}[f] \rangle = f(0) = \langle 1, \mathcal{F}[f] \rangle\). Since this holds for all \(f\), we get 

\[
(-k_{0}^{2} + k\cdot k - m^{2}) \mathcal{F}^{-1} [\phi] = 1
\]

as a distribution

Solving this ends up leading to the propagators that show up in quantum field theory. There's lots of cool stuff there.
